**TASK: Research and develop a presenter-led true-crime YouTube format for a small media team**

I am developing a true-crime video series for a media company. The production model will likely consist of:

* one host/editorial lead/scriptwriter  
* one research producer  
* a remote video/AI production team  
* rotating external experts where appropriate

We are **not** trying to replicate expensive Netflix/48 Hours-style documentary production. The aim is a high-quality, deeply researched, presenter-led YouTube show that can realistically be produced by a small team.

**Reference formats/channels to analyze:**

* Sasha Sulim  
* Elena Pogrebizhskaya / «Без сахара»  
* «Холод» / Holod true-crime investigations  
* Ray William Johnson's true-crime content — use primarily as a contrasting example of a cheaper, faster, personality-led model

Analyze the specific videos/channels provided separately where possible, rather than relying only on general descriptions of the creators.

**Editorial direction we're considering:**  
&nbsp;The series should not focus primarily on famous serial killers or heavily overexposed cases. We are interested in individual crimes, disappearances, relationships, family crimes, fraud/deception, unusual investigations and other cases where the central question is not merely *what happened?* but **why did the people involved behave as they did?**

The crime provides the narrative; **human behavior, psychology, relationships, investigation and the legal process provide the analysis.**

Different episodes may therefore use different experts: forensic/criminal psychologists, psychiatrists, former prosecutors, defense attorneys, detectives, forensic specialists, domestic-violence/coercive-control experts, academics, etc.

**Research questions:**

1. Analyze successful presenter-led true-crime formats on YouTube, particularly formats achievable by small teams. Include the reference channels above plus other relevant successful examples.  
2. Break down their typical episode structure: hook, setup, chronology, reveals, presenter sections, narration, expert material, archive, documents, graphics, conclusions, etc.  
3. Estimate the production/research requirements behind each type of format. What work needs to happen before a script can responsibly be written?  
4. Identify what makes the strongest formats feel **journalistic and well-researched rather than like a simple crime recap.**  
5. Analyze how experts are used. Are they interviewed specifically for episodes? Are existing expert interviews/research quoted? How frequently are experts necessary?  
6. Research common practices around obtaining expert commentary for media: when experts typically appear without fees, when contributor/expert fees may be expected, and realistic ways a small media organization can build a rotating expert pool.  
7. Identify major weaknesses/clichés in the current true-crime YouTube market. What formats, subjects and storytelling devices are oversaturated?  
8. Identify potential differentiation opportunities for a series centered on **“why did this happen?” / understanding the human behavior behind a crime**, without sensationalizing offenders or amateur-diagnosing people.  
9. Compare the viability of approximately 15–20 minute, 25–35 minute and 40–60 minute episodes for this production model.  
10. Determine what a research producer would realistically need to deliver for each episode. Design a standardized **case dossier/research package** that an editorial lead could use to construct the final story.  
11. Identify legal/editorial risks relevant to US-based true-crime production: allegations involving living people, pending cases, defamation, use of court records, copyrighted footage/photos, AI-generated visuals/re-creations, victim privacy, and distinguishing established facts from allegations.  
12. Propose **3 distinct show concepts** compatible with these constraints. Do not merely give three titles for essentially the same show. Each concept should have a genuinely different editorial mechanism.

**For each proposed concept provide:** premise; what makes it different; ideal case types; episode structure; approximate runtime; host's role; research producer's role; use of experts; visual approach; production requirements; expected research burden; major risks/limitations; and an example of what one hypothetical episode could look like.

**Important constraints:**  
&nbsp;Do not assume a large production budget, dedicated investigative team, travel, expensive reenactments or access to exclusive police material. Do not design a format that requires the host to personally perform all research, production and expert booking.

**Research standards:**  
&nbsp;Use current sources. Prefer primary sources, creator/channel information, reputable journalism, industry sources and credible legal/media guidance. Clearly distinguish verified information from inference. Provide source links for substantive claims. Do not invent production budgets, staffing arrangements or behind-the-scenes information that isn't publicly documented; label estimates as estimates.

**Final deliverable:**  
&nbsp;Produce a structured research report that can be used to develop an internal pitch. End with:

* recommended minimum team and division of responsibilities  
* proposed research workflow from case selection to final fact-check  
* standardized case-dossier template  
* the 3 format concepts  
* key decisions management must make before commissioning a pilot  
* a list of questions we should ask management about budget, language/audience, frequency, staffing and expectations.
